Syllabus

Applied Data Science & Visualization · CDAE 7990 · Fall 2026

Course Description

This course introduces students to the foundations of data science and visualization as tools for making sense of complex social, economic, and environmental questions. Students will learn what data are, where they come from, and how different data types shape the kinds of questions we can ask and answer. Using the R programming language as the primary tool, students will practice cleaning, transforming, and organizing data for analysis, with an emphasis on reproducible workflows and clear documentation. While the course focuses on R, students will also be introduced to alternative software commonly used for data work, such as Python, Stata, Tableau, and Excel.

The course highlights best practices for visualizing data, guiding students through the creation of effective charts, graphs, and maps that communicate clearly to a range of audiences. Students will be introduced to spatial data and basic cartographic principles, using R to create maps that illuminate patterns across communities and places. Throughout the semester, students will practice telling evidence-based stories with data, critically evaluating how visual choices influence interpretation, trust, and decision-making in public and professional contexts.

A core component is a semester-long applied project delivered to an external community partner (see Community Partner).

Student Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

  • Develop fluency with core concepts in data, including data types, common sources of quantitative and spatial data, and key issues related to data quality and ethics.
  • Build practical skills in R for data wrangling, including importing, cleaning, transforming, and reshaping data, as well as writing reusable code to automate repetitive data tasks.
  • Design and critique static visualizations using principles of visual perception, clarity, and accessibility, focusing on how to match chart types to questions and audiences.
  • Work with spatial data in R to produce maps and other spatial visualizations that effectively communicate patterns across geographic units and communities.
  • Construct short, data-driven narratives that integrate text, tables, and graphics to communicate findings to non-technical stakeholders.

What Does This Class Look Like?

Class Format

We meet once a week for three hours in Lafayette Hall L202 (Week 1, Sep 3, is a virtual session — see Schedule — every class after that meets in person). Each class period is built around a central theme, and our time is split between lecture, live coding, and hands-on lab work — expect the balance to shift toward lab time as the semester goes on and the NBRC project takes over. I’ll build in a break (or two) to divide the three hours into distinct chunks.

Attendance Policy

You are required to attend class. Put simply, class is better — and your project team is more functional — when everyone shows up. Starting in Week 2, I will take attendance every class period. Everyone gets one free absence. Beyond that, absences will cost you points on your participation & attendance grade. If you know you’ll miss a class, give me a heads-up in advance rather than after the fact.

General Classroom Expectations

My goal as your instructor is to be supportive and build a learning environment where you can thrive. Your role as a student is to actively engage with the material, contribute to your project team, manage your time well, and embrace opportunities for hands-on practice — this is a lab-heavy course, and you learn R by writing R, not by watching it get written. You’re in charge of your own learning, but you’re never doing it alone.

Required Course Materials

Primary textbook: R for Data Science (2nd ed.) by Hadley Wickham, Mine Çetinkaya-Rundel, and Garrett Grolemund — free online.

Computing: Posit Cloud — the class workspace, pre-loaded with all required packages. You’ll receive an invitation to the space via your UVM email; once accepted, opening the workspace gives you your own persistent copy for the whole semester (compute costs are covered by the instructor).

Grading Policy

You’ll be evaluated through a combination of applied homework, in-class labs and participation, and the culminating NBRC project. Homework focuses on hands-on data wrangling and visualization tasks in R; in-class labs and participation give you chances to practice reading, critiquing, and revising visualizations alongside your peers. The project asks your team to take a real dataset from raw data to a clear, audience-appropriate deliverable for NBRC.

Item Weight
Community Partner Project 70%
Homework / Lab Assignments (5 total) 15%
Participation & Attendance 15%

Homework breakdown

Five assignments, each covering what’s been taught since the previous one, due the week shown:

# Due Topic Weight
HW1 Week 2 Intro to R 3%
HW2 Week 4 dplyr / tidyverse; data import & joins 3%
HW3 Week 8 EDA, ggplot2, and chart type selection 3%
HW4 Week 9 Census data & choropleth mapping 3%
HW5 Week 10 Spatial joins & publication cartography 3%

Each assignment is a Quarto document (hw1.qmd–hw5.qmd) already sitting in your Posit Cloud workspace — open the file for that week, fill it in, and render it. There’s no separate submission step; I review your workspace directly.

Use of Generative AI Tools

Students are expected to use all available tools — including generative AI — to help them succeed in this course. Some of the evaluation in this course focuses on the soundness of programming, and AI tools may be genuinely helpful there. However, other evaluation is assessed visually and orally (in-class presentations, code walkthroughs, design critique), so over-reliance on AI is not recommended — you will need to be able to explain and defend your own work.

Communication

If you need to reach me digitally, email is best. I follow a “one business day” rule for responding to student email: if you send something at 10am Monday, I have until 10am Tuesday to respond; if you send it at 6pm Friday, I have until start of business Tuesday. If more than a business day has passed, feel free to send a polite nudge.

Office hours are by appointment — email me and we’ll find a time. On most class days I’ll also stick around afterward for quick questions; please keep it brief if others are waiting.
